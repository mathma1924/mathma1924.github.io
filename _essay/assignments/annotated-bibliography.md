I located these articles using a variety of keywords and techniques. First, I searched keywords like database, archive, digital, digital humanities often in combination with history on Google Scholar and Virgo. The results I was getting were not what I was looking for so I tried searching elsewhere. I did find out that the Journal of American History encourages reviews of digital projects, so I was able to find some projects there. I searched keywords in the main Civil War journals to see if there were any relevant articles with digital humanities work in my field. That’s where I found a couple papers that were topically close to my research. Then I discovered the Journal of Digital History and searched for any interesting projects there. Lastly, I looked in the Computational Humanities book and found some articles I liked. I also looked at the footnotes of Crystal Eddins’ piece and found another resource. 
	
Belanger, Elizabeth. “‘A Perfect Nuisance’: Working-Class Women and Neighborhood Development in Civil War St. Louis.” The Journal of the Civil War Era, vol. 8, no. 1, 2018, 32–63. https://www.jstor.org/stable/26381502.

Elizabeth Belanger performs a spatial analysis of complaints filed by women with the provost marshal in St. Louis and analyzes ethnic compositions of the neighborhoods. She argues that poor women sought to articulate their vision of a harmonious neighborhood through complaints that targeted perceived political, ethnic, and moral disruption. This project focuses on the same topic of my research, so it is good to see how other people have approached the same archival material. She offers a good example of how to use rhetorical analysis in conjugation with digital methods.

Civil War Governors of Kentucky Digital Documentary Edition. http://discovery.civilwargovernors.org/. Created and maintained by the Kentucky Historical Society.
This website contains digitized, transcribed, and tagged papers from the Civil War governors of Kentucky. One of the unique features of this site is the emphasis on people. This project includes network visualization, which is something I am interested in. Each document maps the people, places, and organizations involved. When you navigate to a certain person’s page, you can see who they were corresponding with and the networks they were a part of. Network analysis is a very cool method that I would like to experiment with.

Eddins, Crystal Nicole. “Fugitivities and Futures: Black Studies in the Digital Era.” in Lauren Tilton, David Mimno and Jessica Marie Johnson, eds., Computational Humanities. University of Minnesota Press, 2024.

Crystal Eddins’ chapter is a thought-provoking piece about engaging with digital media within the framework of Black studies and marronage. I like that this piece has historical scholarship while also thinking about our present moment. Eddins used chi-square tests to determine patterns over time. For instance, she finds that women were more likely to seek refuge with a family member or friend. I am impressed by how Eddins analyzed the runaway slave advertisements in a quantitative yet thoughtful way. This methodology inspires how I want to approach my own work. 

Foote, Lorien and Andrew Fialka. Fugitive Federals: A Public History Investigation of Escaped Union Prisoners, fugitive-federals.tamu.edu, published November 2022. 

This website examines the escape of Union prisoners of war from Confederate territory. Key features include a database with all the information compiled by researchers, feature biographies of certain soldiers, and visualizations of escape routes. One of the things I noticed about this site is that the metadata is very comprehensive. They have added where the soldier was imprisoned, when they escaped, and when and where they arrived within Union lines. I am inspired how I can have orderly metadata in my own project to better tell the story of who was interacting with the provost marshals. 

Glatthaar, Joseph T. “A Tale of Two Armies: The Confederate Army of Northern Virginia and the Union Army of the Potomac and Their Cultures.” Journal of the Civil War Era, vol. 6, no. 3, 2016, pp. 315–46. http://www.jstor.org/stable/26070428. 

Glatthaar examined the demographic characteristics of the major armies of the Civil War using digital quantitative analysis. He sought to determine the economic circumstances of the major armies, so he analyzed what percentage of each sample was poor vs well-off. This article is relevant to my work topically, but also methodologically since he is working with data analysis. He was able to gather a lot of data from service records and pension files to do his analysis. I don’t have access to all that information, so I’m thinking about the possibilities and usefulness of doing this type of demographic analysis. 

Hitchcock, Tim and Robert Shoemaker. The Proceedings of Old Bailey. https://www.oldbaileyonline.org/. Digital Humanities Institute at the University of Sheffield. 

This is a massive project that has digitized trial records from Old Bailey and has created a transcription and tagged the records. This site offers an example of how digital humanities methods can be applied to legal records. This data allows researchers to categorize different crimes, the verdicts and the age of the accused. Using this data, you can generate tables with the statistics you want. This project is an example of what someone could do with the court martial cases. Right now, there is a massive spreadsheet but it is difficult to analyze the data in that form.

Hoehne, Patrick. “‘Murderous, Unwarrantable, and Very Cold’: Mapping the Rise of Extralegal Collective Killing in the United States, 1783-1865.” Sec. Jdh002. Journal of Digital History, no. jdh002 (April 2022). https://journalofdigitalhistory.org/en/article/8pGzPyTDKBjR.

Patrick Hoehne uses geospatial analysis and data visualization to argue that extralegal violence was “a form of violent social communication.” I really liked this format in the Journal for Digital History because it combines narrative, data visualization, and hermeneutics. Hoehne charts extralegal violence over time using databases, graphs, and maps to illustrate patterns. For instance, Hoehne demonstrates the rise in violence after 1848 with the increasing political instability. I am inspired by this article to think about how to craft a discrete project that would allow me to do a similar type of analysis. 

Johnson, Jessica Marie. "Markup Bodies: Black [Life] Studies and Slavery [Death] Studies At the Digital Crossroads." Social Text, vol. 36, no. 4, (2018): 57 - 79, doi.org/10.1215/01642472-7145658.

Jessica Marie Johnson argues that digital humanists must engage with Black digital practice to subvert the perceived objectivity of data. Johnson juxtaposes more traditional academic attempts to assemble data on slavery with the grassroots Black digital practices that engage with social justice initiatives. Johnson calls for scholars to engage with digital methods by “remixing conceptual, discursive, and archival geographies, with deliberate, pained intimacy, and, likely, some violence” (71). I am inspired to approach my archive in a similar way, because it contains violence, enslavement, and unfree labor. It’s important to consider how to account for archival violence and silences. 

Janney, Caroline E. Letters Home. https://community.village.virginia.edu/lettershome/. Maintained by the John L Nau III Center, University of Virginia. 

This is a digital project built on John Nau’s collection of over 13,000 Civil War letters. Students at the University of Virginia transcribed and tagged them as well as writing biographies of many of the correspondents. This project has a very good search feature because the documents are well categorized and tagged. You can search by date, location, person and thematic tags. This site offers me another example of metadata. One thing this site does is number every document, which is something I am doing. Letters Home is also a very text-based site. 

Ural, Susannah J. Civil War and Reconstruction Governors of Mississippi. https://cwrgm.org/. 
Maintained by the University of Mississippi Department of Archives and History and the Mississippi Digital Library.

This project digitizes, transcribes, and categorizes the papers of Mississippi’s state governors. This project is very useful for thinking about subject headings and tagging. The creators of this project do a great job with thematically organizing the material in a user-friendly way. I am interested in the social categories they use. For instance, you can browse different religious, social, or political groups. One category that stood out to me was “imprisoned people,” which is important because a lot of correspondence is about appeals for clemency. Thinking about sorting data in this way is useful for my own project, because I want to analyze the patterns based on thematic subsets.
