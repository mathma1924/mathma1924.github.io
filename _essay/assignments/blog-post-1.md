Data injustice reflects the facts that certain knowledge systems and data sets have been privileged while other communities have been marginalized and underrepresented. Machine learning can be used to rebalance this problem. Dr. Patricia Murrieta-Flores demonstrates in her work that research teams can redress some of the imbalance by training models to expand their capabilities and thus creating new data. 

Data injustice is one of the many effects of colonialism in the digital humanities. The historical legacy of exploitation and oppression have created uneven conditions between the Global North and the Global South. Patricia Murrieta-Flores and Shanmugapriya T lay out some of the impediments to data justice in the Global South. One obstacle is that the internet was founded on Western epistemologies, which can obscure and misrepresent non-Western knowledge systems. Also, institutions in the Global South attempting to create digital heritage projects often have less access to financial and technical resources (Murrieta-Flores and T, 4-5). In her talk, Murrieta-Flores shared that the vast majority of training data for commercial large language models are from the Global North. One reason for this is that reading certain scripts is a major difficulty for computers who are not yet trained in that language. The public models in a software like Transkribus demonstrate the disparity among the languages legible to the computer, since Western and Northern European language have more models than Spanish (Murrieta-Flores et al, 3). Murrieta-Flores uses machine learning to address that problem by teaching the computers to more accurately read the data, which allows them to incorporate that information into their program.
	
Patricia Murrieta-Flores offered an example of remedial work with her research, through her projects. Her co-authored article “Unlocking colonial records with Artificial Intelligence” shares the research methodology behind the project. They wanted to develop more handwriting models that reflected the diverse calligraphy styles in the archive. First, they classified each document by calligraphy type using machine learning. Then they trained a handwritten text recognition software to transcribe the documents (Murrieta-Flores et al, 6). They were able to get the error rate below ten percent for most of the models (Murrieta-Flores et al, 12).  Patricia Murrieta-Flores presented on the fleets of New Spain project, which allowed her team to uncover lots of knowledge that was previously obscured by archival silences. They have traced the movement of people and goods across the Atlantic. They have discovered that there were more enslaved Indigenous people than was previously acknowledged. They also traced how the knowledge of plants native to the Americas were adopted by Europeans. 
	
Machine learning is a helpful tool in redressing the historical injustices of colonialism that the digital world is based on. Patricia Murrieta-Flores’ work demonstrates that scholars can successfully use this technology to better represent the world.
