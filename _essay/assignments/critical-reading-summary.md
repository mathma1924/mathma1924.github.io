Critical making is an important part of the digital humanist’s tool kit, and these essays draw out crucial themes. First, making is intrinsic to our work as scholars, allowing us to ask questions which sharpen our analytical skills. David Stanley offered a compelling argument about viewing all interpretive acts as a form of making. Second, it is important to critically engage with the process of critical making. Jeremy Boggs, Jennifer Reed, and J. K. Purdom Lindblad offer case studies for conscientious approaches to critical making. In their book, Anastasia Salter and Emily Johnson offer creative and inspiring ways to approach mapping and generative AI. 

	David Stanley argues that making things is a form of scholarship in “On the “Maker Turn” in the Humanities.” Creating physical objects is a meaningful interpretive act for humanists. Stanley shares examples of his own work, which includes interpreting blocks of text by parts of speech, re-arranging images and texts in different forms, and highlighting key terms in journal. He argues that we have been limited by the textual form and should go beyond that form to 3D objects. There are new possibilities for interpretation beyond the textual. Stanley argues that acts of creation dismissed as “art” actually have humanistic and interpretive value. Scholars are generally blithe about the material labor that goes into the production of their work. He calls physical making “a difference in degree rather than a difference in kind” (Stanley 2017). What he means by this is that it is the same kind of act (interpretation) but a different degree (textual vs physical). Overall, this piece makes a compelling case about making as scholarship 
	
	Jeremy Boggs, Jennifer Reed, and J. K. Purdom Lindblad argue that empathetic understanding should be at the center of inclusive making in “Making it Matter.” They focus on the theory, praxis, and product of making as three different areas that address the major concerns of inclusivity, transparency, and accountability. The authors share how these different areas manifested in three different projects. First, the Makerspace is consciously a place of play and experimentation, allowing users to interrogate the epistemologies they normally engage with. Next, the Take Back the Archive project incorporates feminist and community-oriented design principles to create a user-friendly project. One of the strategies they use is “rich-prospect browsing,” which means focusing on creating accessible browsing for the user. The last example is the Thistlewood project, which mapped the sexual violence enacted on a Jamaican plantation. This project required careful considerations of the violence and archival silences in this research. The creators chose to represent each woman with a different symbol, to emphasize their unique experience. Another interesting decision that they made was to include the source material. This could potentially be controversial as this centers the record written by the enslaver. However, it is important to highlight the source material since it aligns with the key goal of transparency. The authors assert that making is not neutral and it is important to be self-reflective about the choices you make during the process of critical making. 
	
	Anastasia Salter and Emily Johnson empower readers to engage with mapping tools and generative language models to create things. They talk about the importance of critical mapping, since each place has multiple layers of meaning. Thinking about mapping helps us better understand our relationship to the world around us. For example, they draw attention to two maps of Detroit that demonstrate how systemic racism has imperiled residents and impoverished the city. One map tracks all the Black children who were hit by cars and the other shows abandoned buildings (Salter and Johnson 113-114). Salter and Johnson offer instruction on how to create a critical map that focuses on storytelling. They also have a chapter about generative AI. Importantly, they highlight the many ethical concerns including the misappropriation of intellectual property. They argue that the rise of artificial intelligence forces university professors to adapt to new models (230). Salter and Johnson encourage critical engagement with generative AI, through the use of reflection on its output. 
	
	These three pieces all offer perspectives on how to approach critical making. All the authors encourage scholars to look beyond traditional forms of scholarship and experiment with different forms. They also all offer models about how to do critical making in a thoughtful and reflective way. David Stanley demonstrates collaborative work and feedback gathering. Jeremy Boggs, Jennifer Reed, and J. K. Purdom Lindblad demonstrate a values-based approach. Anastasia Salter and Emily Johnson emphasize the importance of self-reflection in the process.
